TL;DRDesigning an antibody loop asks two different questions at once: which positions will touch the antigen, and what amino acid belongs at each of them. Existing models answer both in one head, so antigen signal is spread evenly over positions that mostly do not bind. ConTact answers the first question explicitly, with a supervised contact predictor, and uses its confidence to gate how much antigen information each position receives. That lifts every interface metric on all three splits of ChiMERa-Bench, and the ablations show the gain comes from the cascade rather than from any sequence prior.

Abstract

Computational antibody CDR design methods condition on antigen structure to generate binding loops. Yet, the existing architectures conflate two fundamentally distinct sub-problems: identifying which CDR positions will contact the antigen, and selecting amino acids at those positions. This forces models to learn contact reasoning implicitly through uniform message passing, diluting antigen signal across all positions equally. We introduce ConTact, a contact-then-act architecture that explicitly decomposes CDR design into three cascaded stages: learning surface complementarity fingerprints, predicting CDR-antigen contacts, and injecting contact-gated antigen features into the prediction head. A distance-biased cross-attention module encodes geometric priors favoring spatial neighbors, a contact-weighted cross-entropy loss concentrates gradient signal on binding-critical positions, and a standoff-aware contact-recovery loss places the designed loop against its native epitope. On the ChiMERa-Bench dataset, ConTact attains the best backbone RMSD, fraction of native contacts, interface RMSD, DockQ, and epitope F1 on all three splits, improving native-contact recovery by 11 to 16% relative over the strongest baseline, while matching the best sequence recovery.

Two questions, one head

A CDR design model is handed an antigen, a target epitope and an antibody framework, and has to fill in the loop. That task hides two problems of different kinds. The first is geometric, namely where the loop will touch the antigen, which positions form binding interactions at all. The second is chemical, namely what amino acid belongs at each of those positions, given the pocket it faces.

Current architectures answer both at once. Equivariant graph networks propagate antigen information through uniform message passing that treats every antigen residue alike. Diffusion models concatenate antibody and antigen residues into one flat graph with a fragment-type embedding to tell them apart. In every case a single cross-entropy spreads learning capacity evenly over the loop, so a glycine at the apex that touches nothing is weighted exactly like a tryptophan buried in an antigen pocket.

That matters because the interface is sparse. A CDR-H3 of 10 to 25 residues typically forms only 5 to 15 contacts. The amino acid at a contact position is directly constrained by the chemistry of its partner, while the rest of the loop is constrained mostly by backbone geometry and stability. Spreading the antigen signal evenly spends most of it on the positions that need it least, which is one explanation for a well-documented failure: predictions barely change when the antigen is removed, and BLOSUM substitution matrices explain model outputs about as well as learned likelihoods do.

ConTact answers where before what. A supervised head predicts which positions will contact the antigen, and its confidence then gates how much antigen information reaches each position, both in the forward pass and in the loss.

A three-stage cascade

The encoder is a relation-aware E(3)-equivariant graph network over the antibody-antigen complex, with ten edge types and three virtual nodes. Each virtual node is wired to every epitope residue and every CDR position, which puts any epitope residue two hops from any loop position and removes the bottleneck that sequential message passing otherwise creates.

Cross-attention then combines the loop with the antigen. Plain cross-attention scores a residue 5 Å away and one 30 Å away by feature similarity alone, so ConTact adds a Gaussian spatial bias to the logits, with the distance taken from the coordinates the encoder has just updated,

$$\alpha_{ij}^{(h)} = \operatorname{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{(\mathbf{q}_i^{(h)})^\top \mathbf{k}_j^{(h)}}{\sqrt{D_h}} + \beta_{ij}\right), \qquad \beta_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

The bias is near one inside van der Waals range, falls to about 0.14 at twice the bandwidth, which is roughly the contact threshold, and is negligible beyond three times it. The three decoder stages then run in order.

Stage 1: complementarity fingerprint

A small vector summarizes the binding environment each position faces. It is trained contrastively, with the nearest antigen residue as the positive and distant ones as negatives, so it encodes what kind of pocket a position sits against.

Stage 2: contact prediction

A supervised head reads the position embedding, its nearest antigen neighbours, the distance to the antigen and the fingerprint, and emits a contact probability. A focal loss handles the 3 to 5 times class imbalance between non-contacts and contacts.

Stage 3: contact-gated injection

Local antigen features are added to each position in proportion to its predicted contact confidence, so distant antigen noise is shut out where the model expects no contact and the full local chemistry flows in where it does.

$$\mathbf{h}_i^\text{enriched} = \mathbf{h}_i^\text{cdr} + \hat{c}_i \cdot \text{MLP}_\text{proj}(\mathbf{h}_i^\text{local}).$$

The same confidence reweights the sequence loss, with $w_i = 1 + \alpha\,\hat c_i$ and $\alpha = 3$, so gradient concentrates where the antigen actually constrains the residue. A fifth term, the contact-recovery loss, supervises where the loop sits rather than where each residue lands in absolute space. For every position that contacts the antigen natively, a one-sided hinge pulls its distance to the nearest native epitope partner under the 8 Å cutoff with a 0.5 Å standoff margin, and a 4 Å floor stops the loop collapsing into the antigen,

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{crl} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}} \Big[ \max(0,\, d_k - d_c + \delta) + \max(0,\, d_\text{min} - d_k) \Big].$$

Because it constrains a distance to the binding partner instead of an absolute coordinate, it improves placement without over-constraining the loop's conformation.

Two variants, and what separates them

CDR-H3 inherits a positional signature from V(D)J recombination, strongest at the conserved J-anchor. ConTact-G adds a germline log-prior to the sequence logits, conditioned on the ANARCI-assigned J-gene and the IMGT position and estimated from training counts alone, with a single learnable scale that converges near 0.88 on all three splits. It changes only the logits, so it cannot move the backbone. We report the two variants separately so that the sequence gain and the interface gain are never conflated: the prior buys 3 to 4 points of sequence recovery and leaves the interface metrics within seed noise.

The model has 9.68M trainable parameters, and a single run takes about 2.5 hours on one A100.

The interface separates methods more sharply than RMSD

RAAD and MEAN reach comparable backbone accuracy yet recover 7 to 10 fewer native contacts per hundred, so their loops are correctly shaped and imprecisely placed. The widest gap is on fnat, the fraction of native contacts a design reproduces, which is exactly the quantity the cascade and the contact-recovery loss optimize. Epitope F1 tells the same story from the antigen side: it asks whether the loop contacts the residues it was asked to target rather than an adjacent patch, and ConTact leads it on all three splits.

Sequence recovery is the one axis where the field is flat. ConTact-G reaches 0.40 to 0.41, the best on two splits and within a point of MEAN on the third, while base ConTact sits 3 to 4 points lower. The entire difference is the germline prior. Why no architecture moves this number is the subject of the last section.

The cascade moves the interface

Switching it on raises fnat from 0.565 to 0.593 and epitope F1 from 0.705 to 0.724 at flat sequence recovery. The gain comes from the auxiliary losses shaping the shared encoder, since a disabled contact head emits a constant 0.5 and the contact weighting is then inert.

The contact weight has to be strong

Dropping $\alpha$ from 3 to 2 regresses every interface metric and leaves AAR untouched. A weak reweighting spreads gradient back toward the positions that do not bind.

Exact distances are not a substitute

Replacing the cascade with a distogram loss that matches native interface distances holds fnat and DockQ at the reference and worsens both RMSD and iRMSD, the two metrics it supervises most directly.

the separation that matters

The interface is germline-independent

Removing the prior costs 3 points of sequence recovery and leaves fnat, iRMSD, DockQ and epitope F1 within seed noise. The interface gains owe nothing to the sequence prior, so the two contributions are separable.

Why sequence recovery saturates

Every architecture in the tables lands within a few points of the same sequence recovery, and our ablations move the interface while leaving it flat. Four probes trace both ceilings to a single cause: no model in this setting resolves the CDR-H3 apex backbone better than about 2.4 Å from the framework, the antigen and the masked loop alone.

Recovery of a probe that predicts each residue from geometry alone. Giving it the deposited backbone lifts apex and contact recovery by 15 to 16 points, and shuffling the structure-sequence pairing collapses the gain to the position-only floor.

So the missing sequence signal is real geometry, and it lives in the antibody's own backbone. The trouble is that the predicted backbone is worst exactly where that signal is richest. Breaking the error down along the loop, the apex averages 2.41 Å while the last three J-anchor positions average 1.12 Å, and the structure-to-sequence signal turns net negative once backbone error passes 2 Å. The model builds precisely the segment it cannot then read a sequence from.

Co-design does not break it

A torsion head that rebuilds the loop by NeRF is exact under true internal coordinates, with a round trip of 0.06 Å, yet learns apex torsions only to 47 degrees against the roughly 15 needed, and feeding them to the sequence head changes nothing.

The apex is not retrievable

Superimposing every same-length training loop on the test stem, an oracle picking the best one reaches 2.05 Å median apex error against 2.41 Å for plain mean regression. Ranking by stem similarity gives 7.70 Å, and the oracle loop sits at median rank 38.

The interface rides the same wall

fnat, epitope F1, iRMSD and DockQ are all functions of the same coordinates through an 8 Å contact graph. Tightening the contact-recovery margin trades fnat up and iRMSD down along a DockQ contour pinned near 0.67, so loss reweighting slides along the frontier without moving it.

Position, germline, framework flanks, antigen microenvironment, torsion co-design and library retrieval are each dead ends for the apex. Raising sequence recovery calls for a sharper apex generator or an external evolutionary signal, such as a protein language model, rather than further loss engineering.

That last sentence is the starting point of a companion paper. EvoStruct brings ESM-2 into the sequence head through a structural adapter and recovers the vocabulary that structure-only models lose.
